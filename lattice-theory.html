02 / THE DUAL AND ITS CLASSES

Integer pairings reveal
a finite hidden quotient.

Once a lattice L is integral, ask which vectors have an integer dot product with every vector of L. They form the dual L#, which contains L. The quotient D(L) = L#/L records the finitely many different fractional positions.

For a basis with Gram matrix G, dual coordinates are controlled by G−1. The number of cosets is |D(L)| = det G. If L is even, every coset also has a well-defined squared length modulo 2ℤ: its finite quadratic form q.

Why this matters: a graph may have many vertices, but its forced lattice leaves a finite arithmetic object to check.

03 / THE METHOD IN FOUR MOVES

Make every implication visible.

For a candidate SRG, spectral data constrain a positive semidefinite Gram matrix. Its integer span is the vertex lattice. Integrality and parity then restrict its possible dual classes and even overlattices.

MOVE 01

Choose an eigenspace

The adjacency matrix supplies the eigenvalues and a projection onto a representation space.

MOVE 02

Build the Gram form

A shifted adjacency or scaled projection gives prescribed inner products among vertex vectors.

MOVE 03

Study the dual

Integral pairings place the dual quotient and its quadratic values under arithmetic constraints.

MOVE 04

Test compatibility

Parity, determinant residues, and Milgram’s phase can rule out an impossible lattice—and hence the assumed graph.

Our project’s BMV–Milgram route

In the centred case, the project forms G = b(A − ρI) + cJ. A positive semidefinite G is a Gram matrix; integer combinations of its vertex vectors generate L. The proof then passes to an even overlattice M, studies its finite quadratic form D = M#/M, and uses Milgram’s formula to compare its Gauss-sum phase with the lattice rank modulo 8.

One conclusion, carefully scoped

If all lattices compatible with the graph’s required properties are ruled out, the graph cannot exist. A single failed candidate form is not enough. And ruling out one symmetry only rules out that symmetry; it does not by itself rule out the graph.

04 / GRAPH SYMMETRY BECOMES LATTICE SYMMETRY

A symmetry moves vertices
and preserves their geometry.

A graph automorphism is a permutation of the vertices that preserves adjacency. If its permutation matrix is P, then PᵀAP = A. So it also preserves any Gram matrix built from A and the identity and all-ones matrices.

When the Gram vectors span a Euclidean space, the vertex permutation therefore extends to an orthogonal transformation: it sends each vertex vector vₓ to vg(x), preserving every dot product. Since those vectors generate the vertex lattice L, this transformation sends L to itself. In symbols, the graph’s automorphism group acts by lattice isometries.

What transfers: vertex orbits, fixed vertices, and the group’s linear action become structure on the lattice and its real vector space.

LINEAR ALGEBRA

Fixed space

Vectors unchanged by g form the invariant subspace. Other eigenspaces record how g rotates or reflects.

INTEGER STRUCTURE

Fixed sublattice

The lattice points in that subspace form L ∩ Vg. Their Gram matrix gives integral information about the symmetry.

FINITE GROUPS

Prime order

A finite nontrivial group contains an element of prime order. Ruling out all possible such actions can establish that a graph is asymmetric.

IMPORTANT LIMIT

Symmetry ≠ existence

Showing one automorphism cannot occur says nothing by itself about whether the graph exists without it.

What lattice theory adds

For an element of order 3, the rational vector space splits into a fixed part and a two-dimensional rotation type (the cyclotomic part). On the lattice, each part has its own integral structure, Gram determinant, and dual quotient. Comparing their discriminant data can create incompatible divisibility or residue conditions.

But the rational splitting need not split the integer lattice as a direct sum. The two sublattices can sit inside L with a nontrivial finite index; the missing information is often called gluing. Any determinant comparison has to account for that index and for the induced action on the discriminant groups.

Conway-99: a concrete use

The order-3 argument for srg(99,14,1,2) assumes a symmetry and compares its invariant and cyclotomic lattices. If the action is free, the 99 vertices form 33 triples. If three vertices are fixed, they form a triangle; the remaining vertices form 32 triples, and the invariant calculation uses 35 cells with a weighted inner product.

The weighted fixed-triangle case matters: proving a contradiction only for the free action would leave the fixed case open. The main page walks through the determinant comparison and its scope.

What this method does not automatically give

A lattice isometry need not come from a graph automorphism: it may preserve L while moving its distinguished vertex vectors to other lattice vectors. An abstract automorphism of the discriminant group need not lift to an isometry of L. And ruling out every nontrivial graph automorphism proves asymmetry, not nonexistence. Keep track of which group is acting at each step: the graph automorphism group, the induced lattice isometry group, and the automorphism group of the finite discriminant form are related, but they are not interchangeable.

05 / MILGRAM’S GAUSS-SUM PHASE

A finite form carries a rank checksum.

For a positive-definite even lattice, normalize the sum of exp(πi q(x)) over all discriminant classes. Its phase is fixed by the rank modulo 8. The A₅ example gives a compact exact check: order 6, q(x) = 5x²/6, and rank 5.

Bars show the real and imaginary parts of each term. The normalized sum points in the same direction as the rank-5 target.

THE IDENTITY

|D|−1/2 Σx∈D exp(πi q(x)) = exp(2πi · rank / 8)

This phase is a necessary checksum. The full project test also uses isotropy, a distinguished class, exponent bounds, and per-class conditions.

Unpack the six terms

For x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, use q(x) = 5x²/6 modulo 2. Pairing terms x and 6 − x makes the arithmetic manageable; the normalized sum is a unit complex number with phase 5/8 of a full turn.
