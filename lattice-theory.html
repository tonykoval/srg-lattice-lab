02 / THE DUAL AND ITS CLASSES

Integer pairings reveal
a finite hidden quotient.

Once a lattice L is integral, ask which vectors have an integer dot product with every vector of L. They form the dual L#, which contains L. The quotient D(L) = L#/L records the finitely many different fractional positions.

For a basis with Gram matrix G, dual coordinates are controlled by G−1. The number of cosets is |D(L)| = det G. If L is even, every coset also has a well-defined squared length modulo 2ℤ: its finite quadratic form q.

Why this matters: a graph may have many vertices, but its forced lattice leaves a finite arithmetic object to check.

03 / THE METHOD IN FOUR MOVES

Make every implication visible.

For a candidate SRG, spectral data constrain a positive semidefinite Gram matrix. Its integer span is the vertex lattice. Integrality and parity then restrict its possible dual classes and even overlattices.

MOVE 01

Choose an eigenspace

The adjacency matrix supplies the eigenvalues and a projection onto a representation space.

MOVE 02

Build the Gram form

A shifted adjacency or scaled projection gives prescribed inner products among vertex vectors.

MOVE 03

Study the dual

Integral pairings place the dual quotient and its quadratic values under arithmetic constraints.

MOVE 04

Test compatibility

Parity, determinant residues, and Milgram’s phase can rule out an impossible lattice—and hence the assumed graph.

Our project’s BMV–Milgram route

In the centred case, the project forms G = b(A − ρI) + cJ. A positive semidefinite G is a Gram matrix; integer combinations of its vertex vectors generate L. The proof then passes to an even overlattice M, studies its finite quadratic form D = M#/M, and uses Milgram’s formula to compare its Gauss-sum phase with the lattice rank modulo 8.

One conclusion, carefully scoped

If all lattices compatible with the graph’s required properties are ruled out, the graph cannot exist. A single failed candidate form is not enough. And ruling out one symmetry only rules out that symmetry; it does not by itself rule out the graph.

04 / MILGRAM’S GAUSS-SUM PHASE

A finite form carries a rank checksum.

For a positive-definite even lattice, normalize the sum of exp(πi q(x)) over all discriminant classes. Its phase is fixed by the rank modulo 8. The A₅ example gives a compact exact check: order 6, q(x) = 5x²/6, and rank 5.

Bars show the real and imaginary parts of each term. The normalized sum points in the same direction as the rank-5 target.

THE IDENTITY

|D|−1/2 Σx∈D exp(πi q(x)) = exp(2πi · rank / 8)

This phase is a necessary checksum. The full project test also uses isotropy, a distinguished class, exponent bounds, and per-class conditions.

Unpack the six terms

For x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, use q(x) = 5x²/6 modulo 2. Pairing terms x and 6 − x makes the arithmetic manageable; the normalized sum is a unit complex number with phase 5/8 of a full turn.
