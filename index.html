THE FIRST INGREDIENT

A vector is a step from the origin.

The vector (2, 0) moves two units right. The vector (0, 2) moves two units up. Call these steps b₁ and b₂. Taking any whole number of each step, including negative steps, gives a lattice.

For example, 2b₁ − b₂ = (4, −2). You can reach (4, −2), but you cannot reach (1, 0). Our lattice is L = 2ℤ²: both coordinates must be even integers. The symbol ℤ means all integers, including zero and negative numbers.

How do we measure the steps?

The dot product multiplies matching coordinates and adds:

(x, y) · (u, v) = xu + yv.

The squared length is a vector dotted with itself. So (2, 0) has squared length 4, and (1, 1) has squared length 2. Perpendicular steps have dot product 0.

A basis is a set of independent steps generating the whole lattice. Its size is the rank. This lattice has rank 2.

The Gram matrix records the geometry.

Make a table of the dot products of the basis vectors. The diagonal records their squared lengths; the other entries record how they meet. This table is the Gram matrix.

Two arithmetic properties matter.

An integral lattice has whole-number dot products between every pair of lattice vectors. An even lattice is integral and every vector has even squared length.

Our grid is even: a vector (2a, 2b) has squared length 4a² + 4b². “Even” refers to squared lengths, not to the number of points or dimensions.

The lattice determinant is the determinant of a basis Gram matrix. It is the square of the fundamental cell’s volume. In two dimensions, that volume is an area. A different lattice basis gives the same determinant.

Try adding a dual point

Choose a point. Can we add it to L and keep an even lattice?

All three pair integrally with the original lattice. Their squared lengths tell us which can be added evenly.

The dual asks a different question.

A vector belongs to the dual lattice L# when its dot product with every original lattice vector is an integer. Checking a basis is enough.

For (x, y), our basis gives the conditions 2x ∈ ℤ and 2y ∈ ℤ. So x and y may be half-integers: L# = (½ℤ)². For example, (½, 0) is in the dual because its dot products with b₁ and b₂ are 1 and 0.

A dual vector need not itself have integer squared length. That is why taking the entire dual usually does not produce an even lattice.

Infinitely many points, finitely many classes.

Say two dual points are equivalent when their difference lies in L. They then occupy the same position in different repeating cells. Such a class is called a coset.

Representatives can be chosen with each coordinate in {0, ½, 1, 1½}. There are 4 × 4 = 16 classes. This finite group is the discriminant group D(L) = L#/L.

For an integral lattice, the number of classes equals its determinant. Here both are 16. The quotient notation means we identify points differing by a lattice vector; it does not mean dividing their coordinates.

Keep the remainder of each squared length.

Because L is even, moving a dual point by a lattice vector changes its squared length by an even integer. Its squared length modulo 2 therefore depends only on its coset.

This is the finite quadratic form q on D(L). It remembers more than the number of classes. For example, q(½, 0) = ¼, q(1, 0) = 1, while q(1, 1) = 2 ≡ 0 modulo 2.

A nonzero class with q = 0 is called isotropic. It is an opportunity to enlarge an even lattice.

Add an allowed point, then all its translates.

Adding (1, 1) gives an even overlattice M: all integer points whose coordinates have the same parity. It has a basis (1, 1), (1, −1), with Gram matrix [[2, 0], [0, 2]].

There are two cosets of L in M: the old points and the new points. Its cell area halves from 4 to 2, and its determinant drops from 16 to 4. In general, an enlargement of index t divides the determinant by t².

The project enlarges until no further even enlargement is possible. Such a maximal even overlattice has an anisotropic discriminant form: only the zero class has q = 0. “Maximal” does not imply that its discriminant group is trivial.

Why does this particular enlargement stop?

M#/M has four classes, represented by (0, 0), (½, ½), (½, −½), and (1, 0). Their q values are 0, ½, ½, and 1. None of the three nonzero classes has q = 0, so M admits no further even enlargement in this plane.

The general principle is that adding representatives of an isotropic subgroup gives an even overlattice. Here the subgroup generated by the class of (1, 1) has order 2; the single-vector check is sufficient.

NOW CONNECT IT TO OUR METHOD

A graph would have to supply all this arithmetic.

  1. Assume the graph exists.

    Its vertex degrees and common-neighbour counts impose a matrix identity on its adjacency matrix A.

  2. Turn vertices into vectors.

    A suitable matrix G built from A is a Gram matrix. There is one vector for each vertex, with only two inner products between distinct vectors: one for edges, one for nonedges. The vectors may live in far fewer dimensions than there are vertices.

  3. Take their integer span.

    Choose G with integer entries and even diagonal. Integer combinations of its vertex vectors form an even lattice L. Graph identities constrain its rank and its dual vectors, and hence its discriminant form.

  4. Compare the remaining possibilities.

    A quick case can fail a determinant congruence. The full method passes to a maximal even overlattice M and tests its finite quadratic form. Milgram’s formula requires its Gauss-sum phase to equal the rank modulo 8; further graph-specific conditions must also hold.

  5. Exclude every allowed arithmetic possibility.

    If none works, the assumed graph cannot exist. If one survives, the method is inconclusive: an allowed finite form does not construct a graph.

A REAL EXAMPLE · (169, 42, 5, 12)

Why 1 cannot equal 3.

Suppose a graph with these parameters exists. Our centred construction would give an even lattice of rank 42 whose determinant is a power of 13.

Every power of 13 is 1 modulo 4. But the standard even-lattice rule says an even lattice of odd determinant and rank 42 must have determinant (−1)21 ≡ 3 modulo 4.

The determinant cannot have both remainders. So the required lattice cannot exist, and neither can the graph. This is the project’s parity obstruction, a simple case of the lattice approach. [2]

Where do rank 42 and the prime 13 come from?

The restricted adjacency eigenvalues are 3 and −10, with multiplicities 126 and 42. Use G = −13(A − 3I) + 3J. It vanishes on the all-ones vector and the 3-eigenspace, and acts by 169 on the −10-eigenspace. Thus G is positive semidefinite of rank 42, has even diagonal 42, and satisfies G² = 169G.

The centred dual-vector identity gives 169L# ⊆ L. Therefore every discriminant class is killed by 169, so the discriminant group has order a power of 13. Its order is det L. Here G has 169 rows and is singular: we use its rank and generator identities, not its zero determinant as a generator Gram matrix.

You now have the vocabulary. The next chapter turns graph edges into dot products. Later, the Milgram lab shows how the finite form contributes complex arrows whose sum must point in the direction prescribed by the rank.

An srg(v, k, λ, μ) has v vertices, degree k, and exactly λ or μ common neighbours depending on whether a pair is adjacent. Those local counts become one matrix identity.

Djematene: integer vectors inside an eigenspace.

The eigenlattice Λθ consists of integer vectors x satisfying Ax = θx. Its dual Λθ# consists of vectors pairing integrally with all of Λθ.

Λθ = ℤv ∩ ker(A − θI)

Our project: project the vertices into that space.

Project the standard coordinate vectors using Eθ, then scale to obtain an integral Gram matrix with even diagonal. Their integer span is the centred vertex lattice L.

L ≅ √e · Λθ#

This identity applies to the centred representation G = eEθ. The full project method also uses representations with a nonzero all-ones component. [1, 2]

Show the projection formula

If θ′ is the other restricted eigenvalue, then

Eθ = (A − θ′I)/(θ − θ′) − (k − θ′)J/((θ − θ′)v).

For a primitive sublattice of ℤv, projection of ℤv onto its rational span is its dual. This is the bridge between the two constructions.

03 / DJEMATENE’S 2-ADIC OBSTRUCTION

One remainder
can rule out a graph.

Under explicit hypotheses, an eigenlattice is even with odd determinant. Its rank and determinant must then agree modulo 4. Djematene determines enough of the determinant to test that agreement from the parameters alone. [1]

Why the test works, and when it cannot decide

Write d = r − s. The theorem requires integral restricted eigenvalues, odd d, and ν₂(k − θ′) ≥ ν₂(v). The notation νp(n) means the exponent of p in n. The prime-to-d determinant factor is

Pθ = ∏ qνq(v), over q | v, q ∤ d, νq(k − θ′) < νq(v).

If every prime divisor of d is 1 modulo 4, the unknown d-supported factor contributes 1 modulo 4. The necessary test is (−1)mθ/2Pθ ≡ 1 (mod 4). Odd rank is already impossible under the theorem’s hypotheses. If d has a prime congruent to 3 modulo 4, its unknown exponent prevents this determinant shortcut.

The phase check is only one part of the theorem

GUIDED PROOF

Why an order‑3 symmetry cannot survive

Project audit: order‑3 proof checked
What if three vertices are fixed?

The fixed set is either empty or a triangle. With three fixed vertices, there are 32 moving triples and three singleton cells: 35 invariant cells in total. The invariant form is weighted by diag(3,…,3,1,1,1). Fixed coordinates in the relevant eigenlattice are divisible by 3; the proof rescales its Gram matrix by 1/3 and obtains the same incompatible discriminant residues. This weighted argument is essential; the free-action diagram alone does not cover it.

243 VERTICES · CONSTRUCTION

Change a graph instead of excluding one.

Fix a triangle in the Berlekamp–van Lint–Seidel graph. Its 180 far vertices have a second solution to the prescribed row constraints. Replacing those rows constructs a new graph. Two further examples use changes of voltages in 3-fold covers.

The project verified all three supplied graphs: parameters (243,22,1,2), with automorphism orders 4320, 864, and 3888. These are a separate construction result.

Keep the conclusions separate. Excluding an automorphism rules out that symmetry, not the graph. The deposit’s overall asymmetry claim depends on all prime-order exclusions, including the computer-assisted order‑2 result. Conway‑99 existence is not settled by this website.
